# Boardroom Mode: How Real Board Meetings and Investor Pitch/Q&A Sessions Work

**Purpose.** Domain research to ground the interaction design of Spar's "Boardroom" mode — the user presents to a demanding board/investor panel of 1–3 people who interrupt, challenge, and build follow-up chains on the substance of what is being presented. Everything below is drawn from venture-capital and fundraising practice writing, board-member memoirs and guidance, pitch-prep guides, and executive communication/media-training coaching literature, with sources cited. Items marked *(inference)* are reasoned syntheses rather than direct observations. The document ends with the recommended **mode interaction contract**.

**Compiled:** 2026-09-24. Companion docs: `2026-09-24-mode-defense.md` (3000 ms patience, no presentation interruptions), `2026-09-24-mode-drill.md` (500 ms, mechanical cut-ins), `2026-09-24-mode-open-spar.md` (500 ms, rare one-line build-ons). Boardroom must sit in its own corner of that space: **the most adversarial mode, and the only one where panelists barge into the user's speech on substance** — while still granting the deliberate thinking pause that the roadmap earmarks for it ("tolerance for thinking pauses", `2026-09-24-gemini-live-voice-engine-design.md` §Phase 0; state machine "present <-> interrupt/answer/resume", `2026-09-12-spar-low-latency-architecture-explorer-design.md` §8.2).

## 1. The room: who probes whom, and the power asymmetry

**The presenter** is there to *earn* the room's time and money; the panel is under no obligation to be polite, and everyone knows it. Summarizing a week of viral founder confessions: "the fundraising process is opaque, the power dynamic is real" (TC-Horror). The same asymmetry exists inside the boardroom, where the CEO presents to the people who can replace them. This is the emotional payload Boardroom mode must reproduce: the user should feel that the panel's time is the scarce resource and that goodwill is contingent, not assumed.

**The panel is prepared.** In a real partner meeting, "most partnerships have the point partner write an investment memo ahead of time, which the rest of the partners read" — memos run 3–30 pages even at seed, backed by 5–15 diligence conversations, sometimes consolidated into 24 hours (Wessel). Board members similarly receive the deck "three or four days in advance… so that the Board can start to think about [the discussion items] in advance" (AVC-2012). Design consequence: the simulated panel should behave as if it has read the user's material — never ask "what is this?" but "on slide 7 you claim X — walk me through that."

**The panel is busy and time-boxed.** A partner meeting "usually lasts 60 minutes" with 5–15 investors present, many of whom "have likely been in back-to-back meetings all day" (Wessel). Board meetings run 2–3 hours: "two hours is too short. But more than three hours of intense discussion will turn most brains to mush" (AVC-2012). In both formats the clock is explicit and enforced.

**Engagement is the currency.** The most counterintuitive norm for presenters — and the single most valuable thing to simulate: **challenge = interest; politeness = pass.** "If you're not getting questions, you're not getting money… If an investor is passively nodding in agreement with everything you say, they either don't know or care about the problem you're solving, or they've already decided to pass" (Houck). And from the investor side: "being interrupted with questions is often a good sign, as it means the partners are engaged and interested" (Wessel). The failure state of a Boardroom session is not hostility; it is the panel going quiet *(inference from Houck + Wessel; both directions reported)*.

## 2. How examiners actually probe

### 2.1 Follow-up chains that build on each other

Real questioning is not a list; it is a *chain* — each follow-up is built from the previous answer:

- **"The question behind the question" (QBQ).** "Oftentimes, investors will ask one thing, but the answer they are looking for is a layer deeper… So founders should think about what the investor is really asking, and if they think they know what they're getting at, they can even play it back" ("I think you are asking about…") (Wessel). Q&A prep guides operationalize this: "Answer the question behind the question, not just the literal one" (SU-QA).
- **Risk-targeted chains.** Questions cluster into a small set of risks — "why now, why you, is it real, and where the money goes" — and "every follow-up is a probe into one of them, and… the investor is really running a four-part risk audit and watching which part you flinch on" (StartWise).
- **The fumble is the target.** "The question you can't answer in one clean sentence is pointing straight at a slide you never finished deciding… A founder cruises through nine questions, then hits 'what's your wedge versus the incumbent?' and starts stacking qualifiers" (StartWise). Experienced examiners *find the seam and stay on it*: "a basic [question] they'd written a slide about but never actually decided. That gap is exactly what a sharp investor is hunting for" (StartWise).
- **Out-of-order drilling.** Practice advice mirrors real behavior: "Drill with a partner firing questions out of order until answers are reflexive" (KYN). Oren Jacob's rehearsal method (quoted in Wessel): "by slide 2, they have to interrupt and ask a question that pertains to slide 12… continue to pepper you with questions out of order."

### 2.2 Numbers-chasing

Numbers questions are the spine of hard Q&A, and they are fired *without warning* and expected answered *instantly, from memory*:

- "Investors test founders by asking for CAC, margins, and runway without warning. Answering instantly, with no notes, is one of the cheapest credibility wins in a pitch… When an investor asks about your CAC and you hesitate or reach for notes, it can weaken confidence" (KYN).
- The examiner then attacks the number's *construction*: know "how each number is calculated and what it excludes" — e.g., "Quoting a blended CAC that hides how expensive paid acquisition really is" is a named trap; "Mismatches [between deck and spoken numbers] read as sloppiness or dishonesty" (KYN).
- Then the examiner *stresses* the number with hypothetical derivatives: "Learn the derivatives: if burn rises 20% or growth halves, what happens to runway? Investors probe with hypotheticals" (KYN).
- Board members chase numbers too, but for diagnosis, not diligence: a veteran board member reports the most frequent board-room sentence as "too much of details here … let us get to the numbers," paired with the dictum "Numbers are symptoms. You need to discuss the disease" (AVC-2012, comments). I.e., the chain at a board level is: number → trend → *cause*: what is driving it (AVC-2012).

### 2.3 "Explain the assumption behind that"

The signature move of both VCs and directors is decomposing a claim into its assumptions:

- "They'll ask precise questions about aspects of your business model and question your assumptions. They want to know that you've truly thought things through" (SeedLegals).
- Bottom-up enforcement: "A top-down analysis ('The global cybersecurity market is $200B…') is a rookie mistake. Investors want a bottom-up TAM calculation" — number of customers × ACV, with the capture assumption stated (SU-QA).
- Assumption tests come pre-formulated by investors themselves: "What core assumptions/questions will you test?" — framed with the Airbnb example: prove the premise before claiming product-market fit (SeedLegals, Oliver Kicks).
- Self-awareness probes serve the same function at the person level: "What do you perceive to be your biggest challenge following this raise?" — "If someone doesn't think they will have challenges, it usually isn't a good sign" (SeedLegals, Oliver Kicks). "What's your biggest weakness?" is "not a question about weakness but about honesty, integrity, and authenticity" (SeedLegals, Yana Abramova). "What could kill this business?" — "Investors know the risks; the test is whether you do. Dodging this question is a signal you don't know the business" (SU-MEET).
- Hypothetical, leading, and false-premise forms are standard examiner tools (documented for adversarial interviews, and the direct ancestors of VC "what if Google does this?"): hypotheticals invite speculation ("the headline is your answer"); leading questions "include an assumption — often a harmful one — right in their structure"; false-premise questions "present fiction as fact — correct before you answer" (Moxie).

## 3. Interruption norms: when they cut you off vs. let you finish

This is the starkest difference between the boardroom and every other format Spar models:

- **In 1:1 investor meetings, questions arrive along the way** and the founder guides the conversation (Wessel). **In partner meetings, interruption is the norm**: "you should be prepared to get all kinds of questions throughout your entire pitch, even if it's unrelated to the slide you're presenting. And take it as a good sign! It's not just the clip of questions, but the interruptions that I've seen throw founders off the most" (Wessel). Multiple partners "have often already identified some of the points they want to dig into" from the memo — they are not waiting for the right slide.
- **What triggers a cut-off** (converged across sources; each trigger is a *substance* failure, not a style offense):
  - **Vagueness.** "Vague answers kill more deals than weak metrics do" (SU-MEET). Founders who "show up with such low energy and speak in vague concepts" lose the room (Wessel).
  - **Overclaiming.** Big-market and moat claims without bottom-up support draw immediate challenge (SU-QA §6–7; SeedLegals).
  - **Dodging.** "You didn't answer the question" is the examiner's basic corrective move; repeated dodging is the classic escalation trigger (Moxie on interrogation: "They'll ask the same thing in different ways"; SU-MEET on dodging as a signal).
  - **Detail-dumping / wrong altitude.** Board members cut recitation: "too much of details here… let us get to the numbers" (AVC-2012); the failure mode is "spending too much time on one section and losing interest from the group" (Wessel).
  - **Repetition / no new substance** — the monologue that circles (Wessel's clock-management warning; StartWise's "stacking qualifiers").
- **What earns the floor back:** directness and progress. An answer that is on-topic, specific, and advancing gets listened out — panels don't interrupt for sport, they interrupt to *spend the clock efficiently* (Wessel, clock section; "get to the substance in the first minute or lose the room," SU-MEET).
- **Interruptions are questions, not speeches.** The interrupter cuts in to ask, redirect, or correct — then hands the floor back. Panel members who speech-make mid-presentation are a pathology, not a norm *(inference; consistent with Wessel's description of interruptions as questions and with cross-examination norms cited in the Open Spar research)*.
- **The extreme end of the distribution** (calibration for the mode's ceiling, not its default): partners falling asleep mid-pitch (12-person room, GP "out cold for 30+ minutes. Nobody acknowledged it" — TC-Horror), scowling, walking out mid-meeting (Kalanick's passenger-seat pitch, TC-Horror), and personal-edged provocations ("a charitable read was it was a test of my character" — Prince on Khosla, TC-Horror). Spar's Boardroom should capture *disengagement as the ultimate negative signal* rather than literal sleeping.

## 4. Thinking pauses: tolerance and the strategic use of silence

- **A beat of thought is legitimate and reads as competence.** "When a hard question comes up, don't rush to fill the silence right away. A pause of one to two seconds looks like you're thinking, not avoiding. It gives your brain a chance to switch from reacting to planning" (Moxie). So the panel's *default* patience for a post-answer pause is on the order of 1–2 seconds before anyone expects more speech (either from the presenter or the next question).
- **But numbers are expected instantly.** There is no 1–2 s grace on "what's your CAC?" — "hesitate or reach for notes" and confidence weakens (KYN). Patience is *content-contingent*: deliberative pause on a strategy question = composure; pause on your own headline metric = weakness.
- **Examiners weaponize silence.** Adversarial interviewers "use silence as a way to pressure you" (Moxie). The boardroom equivalent is the flat stare after a weak answer — the panel deliberately does *not* rescue the presenter. A Boardroom simulation should let silence sit for 2–3 seconds after a bad answer before the next (harder) question lands *(inference; direct statement of the tactic in Moxie, boardroom flavor inferred)*.
- **The trained response is refusal to be rushed.** "Refusing to be rushed into answers that require more care than the pace allows" is a named executive skill (Moxie). This is exactly the skill the roadmap wants Boardroom to train: *tolerance for thinking pauses* under pressure.

## 5. Pressure escalation: polite → direct → hostile, and what moves you up or down the ladder

A three-level ladder is directly attested in the interrogation literature and fully consistent with investor/board accounts:

- **Level 1 — curious/probing.** Clarifications, "walk me through," follow-ups on specifics; tone is engaged, even warm (Wessel: most partners are "curious and friendly"; her own fear of "gotcha questions" turned out overblown).
- **Level 2 — direct/challenging.** Premise attacks and re-asks: "I'd push back a little on that framing" (Moxie); repeating the same question in different words (Moxie); "That's not what I asked"; demanding the number itself, not the narrative (KYN, SU-MEET).
- **Level 3 — adversarial/hostile.** "Some interviewers — especially in investigative or adversarial situations — will push, repeat, and escalate. They'll ask the same thing in different ways. They will use silence as a way to pressure you. They'll mischaracterize what you said before and ask you to respond" (Moxie). Boardroom equivalents: open doubt on the record ("that answer worries me"), the loaded hypothetical, and — at the real extreme — personal tests and humiliations (TC-Horror; VCC). For Spar, Level 3 means sharply personal-adjacent pressure *about the business* ("I've heard this pitch five times this year — tell me why yours isn't the same") — never demeaning the person.

**What escalates** (triggers, converged): dodging or a second non-answer on the same question (Moxie: repeat-and-escalate; SU-MEET: dodging is a signal); defensiveness — the single fastest route down: "She answered defensively, which spiralled into a 10-minute debate that buried her message… 'I lost them the moment I got defensive'" (WP-Bridge); inconsistency between spoken numbers and materials — "read as sloppiness or dishonesty" (KYN); overclaiming exposed by a follow-up (StartWise's "seam"); blaming others (the adversity question tests whether you "crumble, blame others, or learn and adapt," SU-QA).

**What de-escalates:** a direct, specific answer to the *actual* question; honest limits — "Say so, commit to a follow-up, deliver within 24 hours. Faking an answer is worse than admitting a gap" (SU-MEET); conceding a fair point without capitulating ("That's a fair point. What matters now is…" — WP-Bridge's acknowledge-bridge-deliver); composure under silence (Moxie); naming your own weaknesses before the panel does (SeedLegals, biggest-challenge and biggest-weakness questions). De-escalation looks like the examiner returning to Level 1 curiosity or moving topics *while engaged*. **The deceptive "de-escalation"** is polite withdrawal: nodding, no pushback, early wrap — the disinterest signal (Houck). A good simulation must make silence-after-politeness feel worse than hostility.

## 6. Multi-examiner dynamics: tag-teaming, champion and skeptic

- **The champion/ally is structurally present.** "If you're in the partner meeting, someone in the room already believed in what you're building enough to put you there… 'There was more than one moment in the call where I forgot to highlight an interesting feature… and this investor intentionally brought it up. It feels like someone is already in your corner'" (Wessel, quoting Gumloop's Max Brodeur-Urbas). But: "even if they're your champion, point partners still want you to stand on your own feet… when appropriate, you may get a nudge from them" (Wessel). And the corollary that defines the pressure: "when they bring you in front of all the partners, they're essentially vouching for you and **the hard questions will likely come from the rest of the team**" (Wessel, quoting Yutong Sun).
- **Tag-teaming and pile-ons.** With 5–15 partners, questioners hand off, re-open each other's threads, and converge when blood is in the water ("one partner… fell asleep & another couldn't stop scowling," Wessel's own Series A, TC-Horror). Multiple examiners deliberately attack from different angles — market, team, math — sometimes in overlapping succession *(inference from Wessel's attendee counts and question sources; standard in partner-meeting accounts)*.
- **Role asymmetry is a real, learnable read:** one skeptic, one ally, one numbers person, one disengaged. The presenter's skill is allocating attention: answer the skeptic directly, take the ally's lifelines, don't perform for the disengaged *(inference; the champion/skeptic contrast is directly attested in Wessel)*.
- **Scaling to Spar's 1–3 panelists:** with 3, assign explicit roles (lead skeptic, champion-who-still-tests, numbers examiner) and rotate who interrupts. With 2, one skeptic + one ally. With 1, the single examiner *time-shifts* roles — curious opening, adversarial middle, procedural close — so the user still experiences the full ladder *(design inference from attested roles)*.

## 7. How meetings open: time-boxing and agenda framing

- **Partner meeting (investor Q&A):** the clock is announced and owned: 60 minutes, slides recommended but "partners want conversation, not a presentation" — "Bring the deck but expect it to be barely used" (SU-MEET). There are "usually… no introductions on the VC side, as that would take up too much time from the pitch" (Wessel). Substance is demanded in the first minute: "'Tell me about the company' — 60 seconds. What you do, who buys it, what the metric that matters is. Not history, not founding story, not vision. Investors are triaging — get to the substance in the first minute or lose the room" (SU-MEET). Don't be late: "It definitely is noticed when the entire partnership is waiting" (Wessel).
- **Board meeting:** the chair/CEO frames scope first — perfunctory official business is disposed of "at the start of the meeting so that the meeting doesn't have to get cut short" to approve options and minutes; then the CEO "tees up" 3–4 discussion topics, "most of the discussion items should be strategic and thorny questions… Note that all of these are questions" (AVC-2012). The modern version: pre-read assumed read ("ideally you would spend that time discussing the pre-read and not presenting it as you should assume and expect everyone will have read it"), 30–60 minutes on the numbers, then 1–2 strategic topics, and an executive session with just CEO and board at the start *and* end (AVC-2025). The "keeping me up at night" list is itself an opening ritual: the CEO puts 3–4 worries on a slide at the start of each meeting, compared against last meeting's list (AVC-2012).

**Design synthesis for the mode's opening:** minimal pleasantries; announce the time box and the ground rules *including that interruptions will happen*; assume the pre-read; demand substance within 60 seconds.

## 8. How meetings close: verdicts and next steps

- **Partner meetings close into a verdict machinery.** "Usually, there's a debrief meeting that happens that same day… partners will re-group to discuss their thoughts and then vote… Most firms will get a decision back to the founder within 24 hours of a PM — some firms try to get back to you within a few hours… you should expect a phone call from the partner" (Wessel). Partner-meeting offer rates are "anywhere from 25–60%" — the room is genuinely undecided, which is why performance matters (Wessel).
- **Board meetings close into feedback, out of the room.** "The executive session at the end of the meeting… when the Board meets without the CEO… After the executive session ends, the CEO should either be invited back to have a debrief… or the Chairman of the Board should meet with the CEO to debrief… This is an opportunity for the Board to provide feedback to the CEO on the business, the team, and performance, and the strategy. Boards should not miss this opportunity… and CEOs should demand it of them" (AVC-2012; restated in AVC-2025).
- **Open items get owners and deadlines.** The norm for unanswered questions: "commit to a follow-up, deliver within 24 hours" (SU-MEET). Feedback after the fact is real and askable: ask the point partner "what were the things they were most excited about, and what were their concerns? If they didn't invest, what held them back?" (Wessel).

**Design synthesis:** the close is a *verdict ritual* — enumerate unresolved concerns, give an explicit signal (proceed / conditional / pass), name next steps with deadlines, and deliver performance feedback. In a practice room the debrief doubles as the coaching surface.

## 9. The rhythm of a single hard exchange

Across sources, one hard Q&A exchange follows a stable four-beat micro-structure:

1. **Challenge** (5–15 s): a specific, sourced question — often referencing what the presenter just said or the material ("on slide 7…", "you said your wedge is X…"). One question at a time.
2. **Answer** (10–30 s): the presenter's turn. Strong answers: direct, one clean sentence first, then the support — "The question you cannot answer in one clean sentence names the slide to fix" (StartWise). A deliberate 1–2 s thinking pause before answering is legitimate (Moxie).
3. **Push-back or follow-up** (the examiner's next move, chosen from the answer's quality):
   - *Resolution path:* answer landed → examiner either concedes in clipped form ("Fair." / "That's the number I was looking for.") and moves on, or drills one level deeper to confirm ("And what's in that CAC?") before moving on (KYN; Moxie's acknowledge step).
   - *Pressure path:* answer was vague/dodging/overclaimed → the examiner **stays on the seam**: restate the original question verbatim, demand the number, attack the premise, or sit in 2–3 s of deliberate silence, then re-ask (Moxie: "push, repeat, and escalate"; "ask the same thing in different ways").
4. **Resolution or yield:** resolution = the examiner visibly banks the answer and pivots to the next topic (or hands off to a colleague). Yield = the presenter concedes honestly ("I don't know — I'll send you the cohort data by Friday"), which *also* resolves the thread (SU-MEET), or the examiner abandons the thread while marking the concern — "we'll come back to that" — which carries it into the close.

The presenter-side counter-structure taught by coaches maps exactly onto beats 3–4: acknowledge ("that's a fair point" — max one sentence), bridge ("what matters more right now is…"), deliver (one message, then stop) (WP-Bridge; Moxie's Pause-Pivot-Power and Flag-Frame-Focus). The defensive spiral is the anti-pattern: "She answered defensively, which spiralled into a 10-minute debate that buried her message" (WP-Bridge).

## 10. Presenter failure modes (what the panel punishes hardest)

Consistent across all sources, ranked roughly by how fast they kill the room:

1. **Not knowing your own numbers** — hesitation or notes on CAC/burn/runway (KYN); "founders who cannot answer basic cost and margin questions lose the room" (KYN, televised pitch shows).
2. **Dodging** — "Dodging this question is a signal you don't know the business" (SU-MEET); triggers repeat-and-escalate (Moxie).
3. **Vagueness** — "Vague answers kill more deals than weak metrics do" (SU-MEET).
4. **Defensiveness** — "I lost them the moment I got defensive" (WP-Bridge); reads as hard-to-work-with *(inference; defensive founders signal board-relations risk)*.
5. **Inconsistency** — deck vs. spoken numbers mismatch "reads as sloppiness or dishonesty" (KYN).
6. **Bluffing** — "Faking an answer is worse than admitting a gap" (SU-MEET).
7. **Wrong altitude** — detail-dump instead of numbers (AVC-2012) or weeds-only technical narration (Wessel, "forest and trees": the best founders "altitude shift").
8. **Fading under silence** — rushing to fill the panel's deliberate pause (Moxie).

## 11. Design implications for Spar's Boardroom mode

1. **Interruption is the mode's identity.** The state machine already specifies "present <-> interrupt/answer/resume": unlike Defense (presentation sacrosanct) a triggered Boardroom panelist *cuts into the presentation itself*. Interruptions must be substantive questions tied to the user's actual last words or their material — never "excuse me" noise. Interruption density itself should be the difficulty dial.
2. **Challenge signals engagement; quiet signals death.** Prompt-level framing: the panel asks hard questions *because* it is interested. If the user performs well, the mode should *intensify*, not soften (Houck; Wessel). Only genuine mastery earns the de-escalated, collegial close.
3. **Follow-up chains of depth 2–3.** One examiner thread = challenge → answer-quality-dependent push-back → resolution/yield (§9). Implement an explicit per-thread state: open / deepening / banked / deferred-to-close ("we'll come back to that"), and carry deferred threads into the closing verdict.
4. **Numbers first, instantly.** The panel demands figures from memory; hesitation on headline metrics is itself an event the panel notices and pounces on (KYN). With Spar's material uploads (roadmap project 4), cross-examine the user's own deck numbers.
5. **Pause asymmetry.** Tolerate the user's deliberate 1–2 s thinking pauses after questions (that's the trained skill), but distinguish them from *hesitation on known-own-numbers*, which draws immediate pressure (Moxie; KYN). The panel also uses silence as a weapon: after a weak answer, 2–3 s of dead air before the next, harder question (Moxie).
6. **1–3 panelists with roles.** Lead skeptic / champion-who-tests / numbers examiner (§6). Handoffs by name ("Priya, you had a question on the pipeline?"). A lone examiner role-shifts through the same ladder.
7. **Extended Thinking maps to in-character deliberation.** The roadmap assigns Boardroom to `gemini-3.8-live-extended-thinking`; the model's pre-response thinking gap can be performed *as* the panel conferring — "the panel exchanges a look" — making latency diegetic rather than a bug.
8. **The close teaches.** The verdict ritual (concerns → signal → next steps with deadlines) doubles as the practice debrief; per AVC, boards owe the CEO a feedback session, so the mode's closing feedback is itself role-authentic.

### Rationale per field

**`endOfSpeechPatienceMs: 1200`** — Boardroom's aggression lives in *when it interrupts* (mid-speech, on substance), not in how fast it jumps on silence. The research draws the patience line by content, not by reflex: a 1–2 second thinking pause "looks like you're thinking, not avoiding" and is the trained executive behavior the mode exists to build (Moxie), while the roadmap explicitly reserves "tolerance for thinking pauses" for Boardroom as its contrast with Drill and Open Spar (both 500 ms) and Defense (3000 ms). 1200 ms sits at the top of the conventional 800–1200 ms endpointing band documented in the Open Spar research: long enough that a deliberate composer's pause survives untouched and the panel's silence can itself feel like pressure (Moxie: interrogators "use silence as a way to pressure you"), short enough that the follow-up still lands with the brisk impatience of a 60-minute partner meeting (Wessel; SU-MEET). It also preserves mode separation: Drill (500) is a buzzer, Open Spar (500) is a volley, Defense (3000) is a chaired hearing, Boardroom (1200) is a panel that lets you think — briefly — because its time is expensive.

**`interruptionPolicy`** — This field is where "most adversarial and most willing to interrupt" is grounded, and every clause is sourced. Barge-in during the presentation is the documented partner-meeting norm: "you should be prepared to get all kinds of questions throughout your entire pitch, even if it's unrelated to the slide you're presenting" — and it's "the interruptions" specifically that "throw founders off the most" (Wessel), which is precisely the exposure a practice room should sell. The triggers are the five converging, substance-based cut-off causes in §3: vagueness ("vague answers kill more deals," SU-MEET), overclaiming without numbers (KYN; SU-QA), dodging (SU-MEET; Moxie), repetition, and detail-dumping at the wrong altitude ("too much of details here… let us get to the numbers," AVC-2012). The "questions, never speeches" constraint keeps interruptions cross-examinative rather than rude — real interrupters want the floor *back in play*, and a talking-over panelist would break the room's credibility. The never-interrupt-progress rule encodes the other half of the norm: panels interrupt to spend the clock efficiently, and a landing answer buys the floor (Wessel's clock section; SU-MEET's "get to the substance… or lose the room"). The escalation clause imports Moxie's "push, repeat, and escalate" ladder gated on repeated dodges, and the final sentence hard-codes the mode's most counterintuitive truth: interruption is engagement, quiet is the pass signal (Wessel; Houck).

**`responseStyleHint`** — Every stylistic element is a documented examiner behavior, not a mood. Terse and numbers-first: investors fire metric questions "without warning" and read instant, note-free answers as credibility (KYN). Chains 2–3 deep on one seam reproduce the attested follow-up structure — question behind the question (Wessel's QBQ; SU-QA), risk-targeted probes with the examiner "watching which part you flinch on" (StartWise), then the derivative hypothetical ("if burn rises 20%… what happens to runway?", KYN) and the decomposition attack ("what it excludes," "blended CAC" trap, KYN). Anchoring to the user's last words or slide reflects memo-prepped panels ("on slide 7…") and the rehearsal doctrine of out-of-order, material-referenced questions (Wessel/Oren Jacob; SeedLegals' "question your assumptions"). Premise challenges and naming the dodge are the direct-level rungs of Moxie's ladder; accepting honest gaps with a follow-up deadline mirrors "say so, commit to a follow-up, deliver within 24 hours" (SU-MEET). The no-praise rule and the clipped "Fair." acknowledge come from the bridging literature's one-sentence acknowledgment cap (WP-Bridge) — in a real hard room, warmth is rationed exactly that sparingly.

**`pacingHint`** — The panel-owned tempo mirrors the power asymmetry of a 60-minute partner meeting where the presenter manages the clock or loses the room (Wessel; SU-MEET). The 10–30 s expected answer length synthesizes "one clean sentence" as the strength bar (StartWise) with the deck-clock pressure to not "spend too much time on one section" (Wessel). The pause asymmetry is the research's sharpest, least-obvious finding: 1–2 s of deliberate thought is legitimate composure (Moxie), but hesitation on your own headline numbers is a credibility event (KYN) — the mode must treat those two silences differently, which no sibling mode does. The 2–3 s deliberate silence after a weak answer operationalizes "they will use silence as a way to pressure you" (Moxie) as a paced, audible beat rather than dead air. Stepwise escalation gated on repeated dodges, with prompt de-escalation after a specific honest answer, follows §5's trigger table (Moxie; WP-Bridge; SU-MEET), and the audibly enforced clock ("twenty minutes left") reproduces both the partner-meeting time box (Wessel) and the board practice of teeing up thorny questions against a hard stop (AVC-2012).

**`openingBehavior`** — Modeled on the partner-meeting opening, with the board variant folded in. Minimal pleasantries and no introductions: "in a partner meeting, there usually aren't introductions on the VC side, as that would take up too much time from the pitch" (Wessel) — in Spar the user already picked the panelists, so names suffice. The explicit time box and interruption ground rule are the authentic frame for a 60-minute meeting (Wessel; SU-MEET's "60 minutes typically") and they do double duty as practice-room consent: the user is told the room is adversarial *before* it is. "We've read your material" reproduces the pre-read norm on both sides of the industry — investment memos (Wessel) and board decks sent days ahead, discussed rather than presented (AVC-2012; AVC-2025) — and licenses immediately material-referenced questions. The 60-second substance demand is SU-MEET's triage rule verbatim in spirit: "get to the substance in the first minute or lose the room," no founding story, no vision tour. Letting the first interruption arrive "as early as the first vague claim" sets the mode's expectations honestly from second one (Wessel).

**`closingBehavior`** — The verdict ritual reconstructs how these meetings actually end. The concern enumeration mirrors what a real committee carries out of the room: the same-day debrief where partners "re-group to discuss their thoughts and then vote" (Wessel), and the board's executive session that converts into chairman-to-CEO feedback (AVC-2012; AVC-2025). The explicit outcome ladder (proceed / conditional / pass) is real: partner-meeting offer rates of 25–60% mean the room is genuinely undecided and the presenter's performance moves the verdict (Wessel) — so the simulation's verdict must be *earned*, not constant. Next steps with deadlines reproduce the decision logistics ("a decision back to the founder within 24 hours… you should expect a phone call," Wessel) and the open-item norm ("commit to a follow-up, deliver within 24 hours," SU-MEET), which also trains the user to close loops like an operator. The final direct feedback line is doubly grounded: boards owe the CEO a debrief "on the business, the team, and performance" (AVC-2012), and a practice room that never tells the user what to fix has wasted the pressure it applied. Naming the uncovered items when the clock runs out enforces the time box to the very end (Wessel; SU-MEET).
