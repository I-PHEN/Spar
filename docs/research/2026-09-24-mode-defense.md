# Defense Mode: How Real Oral Defenses, Vivas, and Committee Examinations Work

**Purpose.** Domain research to ground the interaction design of Spar's "Defense" mode — a student presents, then a committee of 1-3 examiners questions them. Everything below is drawn from university defense guidelines, viva preparation literature, examiner surveys, and oral-assessment research, with sources linked. Items marked *(inference)* are reasoned syntheses rather than direct observations.

## 1. Who is in the room, and what each role does

**The candidate.** Presents, then answers. Expected to behave as "the expert on the thesis topic" — the person in the room who knows this specific work best (Cortazzi).

**Examiners (2 in the UK; a committee of 3-5 in the US and much of continental Europe; up to a dozen on a European public jury).** In the UK model there is a minimum of two: one **internal** (from the candidate's university) and one **external** (from another university), both specialists who have read the thesis in full beforehand (DiscoverPhDs). In US and continental public defenses, committee members each get their own questioning slot. Examiners combine three folkloric roles: "gatekeepers or custodians to preserve standards, discussants to recognize and explore new research, celebrants to laud and reward achievement" (Cortazzi, citing Dalley et al.; Carter; Lovat et al.). Examiners routinely *contribute* points rather than only asking: "the examiners ascertain the candidate's approach and understanding of the work through scholarly and research-based discussion. They themselves contribute interesting points and raise specific ideas, rather than simply asking questions" (Cortazzi).

**The chair.** "Normally, there is a chairperson for the viva, whose main role is to ensure compliance with regulations and that the procedures are smooth and fair; otherwise, the chairperson likely says very little except to give a brief introductory explanation and coordinate the conclusion for the viva" (Cortazzi). University of Cambridge guidance (quoted in Poole) tasks the independent chair with ensuring that "the viva voce examination process is rigorous, fair, reliable and consistent," that "the candidate has the opportunity to defend the thesis and respond to all questions posed by the examiners," and that "questioning by the examiners is conducted fairly and professionally." At US public defenses the chair (usually the advisor or committee chairperson) also runs the room: calls the session to order, manages the audience Q&A window, and dismisses the audience for the closed session (DiscoverPhDs; Thesisroom). The Dutch system externalizes timekeeping to a **beadle**, who ends the defense on the hour with the ceremonial call "Hora est!" (TU Delft doctoral regulations, via Thesisroom).

**The supervisor.** In the UK, may attend only by prior agreement and "is prohibited from asking questions or from influencing the outcome" (DiscoverPhDs). In some countries (Japan, Brazil, Finland, the Netherlands) the supervisor sits on the examining committee itself (Cortazzi).

**The audience (public-defense formats only).** In the US, Netherlands, France, Scandinavia, and elsewhere, colleagues, friends, and family attend the presentation portion and sometimes ask questions (DiscoverPhDs; Cortazzi; Wikipedia's Norway entry: the *disputas* opens with a public lecture where the audience can ask questions, then two designated opponents question the candidate).

**Mapping to a 1-3 examiner sim.** A real UK viva has exactly 2 examiners + chair; a US/continental committee is 3-5. A 1-examiner sim best maps to a *mock viva run by a supervisor* rather than a real defense; 2 examiners is the UK norm; 3 matches a small committee. One panelist should always carry chair duties (opening, handoffs, timekeeping, closing) — in reality a distinct person or the lead examiner doubling up (DiscoverPhDs: "one of the examiners can also take on this role").

## 2. Presentation phase vs. questioning phase, and the transition between them

**Two families of format:**

- **UK/Irish closed viva:** *no presentation at all*. The examiners have read the thesis; the session opens directly with warm-up questions, the first almost always being "tell us about your research" (DiscoverPhDs; Thesisroom).
- **US/continental defense:** a **presentation phase first, then questioning**. US: "a public presentation of twenty to thirty minutes followed by closed-door committee questions" (Thesisroom; the presentation "is not an exam; it is a formal report"). Netherlands: 10 minutes of lay-facing presentation, then 45 minutes of questioning by the opposition committee (Thesisroom). France: 20-30 minute presentation, then extended jury discussion (Thesisroom). Where a talk exists inside the viva itself, it "does not last for more than 10 or 15 minutes, but it can be for 30 minutes in Singapore or 40 minutes in Ghana or Japan" (Cortazzi).

**Transition ritual.** The chair marks the boundary explicitly: thanks the candidate for the talk, states that questioning will now begin (in public formats, first dismisses the audience or announces the rules for audience questions), and hands over — commonly to a designated lead examiner or in a pre-agreed order. Cortazzi notes examiners pre-plan the questioning structure: "Sometimes they make an agenda or sequence of topics. Sometimes they decide in advance who will lead the viva discussion about which aspects. Sometimes they decide to proceed section by section in thesis chapter order, or, more interestingly, by topic or theme." *(Inference: the handover line ("Thank you — a clear presentation. We'll move to questions now; Professor X will start") is the single most recognizable transition marker in the whole event and should be scripted.)*

## 3. How examiners sequence questions

- **Arc.** Warm-ups ("What led you to this topic?", "summarise your thesis") → deep dive in roughly chapter or theme order with "the heaviest questioning in the methodology and findings chapters" → contribution, limitations, and future work in the final third (Thesisroom; DiscoverPhDs; Cortazzi's category order: opening warm-ups → conceptualization → design → methods → outcomes/conclusions → contribution/wider issues).
- **Turn-taking.** "The chair introduces you all and then the examiners take turns asking you questions" (Glasgow). In Scandinavia, "several designated opponents each questioning in turn" (Thesisroom). Panels pre-allocate topics per examiner (Cortazzi).
- **Chains, not checklists.** The single best-documented sequencing fact: "In reality, because of time constraints, only one or two questions from each category are feasible. Importantly, these apparently general questions are commonly followed up with more focussed questions on the same theme. Follow-up questions are related to the specific thesis, sometimes in sustained exchanges of dialogue" (Cortazzi). So the real rhythm is: one broad question → 1-3 sharpening follow-ups drilled into the specifics of the student's answer → explicit or implicit handoff to the next topic or next examiner.
- **Granularity.** Good vivas reference specifics: "turn to specific pages" (Thesisroom). At the pathological extreme, examiners "go through the thesis page by page with cross-checking the details of specific words, sentences and paragraphs" — Cortazzi explicitly labels this the *worst* mode; the best is thematic scholarly discussion.

## 4. Interruptions: during presentation vs. during questioning

- **During the presentation, interruption is not the norm.** The US talk is "a formal report" delivered to the room; audience and committee questions come after (Thesisroom; DiscoverPhDs: the student "presents their work in the form of a lecture and *then* faces questions"). Dutch questioning is strictly separated from the 10-minute presentation by regulation (Thesisroom). *(Inference: interruptions during the talk, where they occur at all, are brief clarification requests ("sorry — which dataset is this?") or time cues from the chair, and a well-run room treats substantive challenges as deferred.)*
- **During questioning, the exchange is conversational.** Follow-ups interleave with answers; examiners redirect drifting candidates. Open hostility is rare and officially disapproved: an experienced examiner reports "in about sixty vivas I have only seen friction about four times," and a senior figure estimates problematic "maverick" examiner conduct in ~5% of vivas (Poole). Chair's mandate includes keeping questioning "fair and professional" (Cambridge guidance, quoted in Poole).

## 5. Responses to vague or evasive answers

- **Rephrase before escalating.** When a candidate's answer misses the question, examiners may first suspect a dodge: "the examiners may not realize what has happened and may think the candidate has dodged the question... **good examiners will patiently rephrase the question and help the candidate to focus on the point in hand**" (Cortazzi).
- **Sustained push.** Follow-up chains "sometimes in sustained exchanges of dialogue" (Cortazzi) — the examiner stays on one point across multiple turns, each probe more specific ("What are the limitations?" → "You didn't mention sampling — why these participants?" → "But couldn't a critic say the results don't travel beyond your two sites?"). *(Inference: 1-3 probes on a single point is the natural unit — enough to demonstrate persistence, rarely more before the examiner notes it for the report and moves on; zero-push sessions read as fake, five-push grilling reads as the ~5% maverick case.)*
- **Evasion is treated as data, not confronted dramatically.** Examiners "await your comments" toward critical views and expect the candidate to "acknowledge a weakness... and speculate on how the weakness might be overcome"; refusing to concede anything is itself penalized: "it is a weakness not to recognize the weakness" (Cortazzi).
- **The candidate is explicitly allowed repair moves.** Asking for clarification or repetition is legitimate; Cortazzi even scripts the candidate's check: "I'm not sure if I answered that question as well as I would like to: would you like me to say more?" — "This gives examiners the cue to re-focus on what they are asking."

## 6. Tolerance for silence and thinking pauses

- Pausing to think is normalized and coached: "If a question is really challenging... you can say, 'That's an interesting question, please give me a moment to think about it'... **This is normal**: it gives you extra thinking time... it is a lot better than rushing to give an answer with less thought" (Cortazzi). Candidate-facing advice repeats it: "Take your time answering. Make a note of the question, take a few moments to think. There's no rush!" (Muller). Typical coached answer length is ~2-3 minutes, not instant-fire (University of Calgary viva advice, via search).
- **Wait-time research quantifies the expert norm.** Teachers on average leave only ~1 second of silence after a question or answer; deliberately extending that pause to **3-5 seconds** measurably lengthens responses, improves accuracy, and raises the cognitive level of answers (Rowe 1972, 1986 — "wait-time I" after the question, "wait-time II" after the student stops speaking). Expert examiners — who are also writing notes between turns, and who are cautioned against conflating "think[ing] on the hoof" with competence ("some people require time and certain conditions to tease through problems," Tinkler & Jackson, quoted in Poole) — sit at the patient end of that distribution.
- Silence also has institutional texture: candidates routinely report the end-of-viva wait ("the longest ten minutes of my life" while the panel confers, Glasgow) and examiners pause to make notes mid-session. A simulation that never leaves silence miseducates students for a room where 3+ seconds of quiet after a question is unremarkable.

## 7. What pass / pass-with-revisions / fail pressure looks like behaviorally

**The outcome ladder (UK, six rungs; US compresses to three).** (1) Pass with no corrections — "rare"; (2) pass with minor corrections — "the most common" outcome; (3) pass with major corrections — "somewhat uncommon"; (4-5) downgrade to MPhil — uncommon/rare; (6) immediate fail — "very rare." "Almost all students who sit their viva pass it" (DiscoverPhDs). US: "pass, pass with revisions, or the rare failure" (Thesisroom).

**Behavioral signatures by trajectory** (from Poole's interviews):

- **Brilliant thesis:** "you start with 'This is brilliant, basically I just want to establish that you wrote it'... but actually you then engage in a really deep intellectual discussion that almost always gets on to publications" (examiner Z). Some panels announce the pass immediately: "there are times when the thesis is so good that the result is announced to the student at the beginning" (interviewee X); in one case an examiner asked about a missing comma on page 45, then said "Congratulations Dr B... You've passed." Tone: relaxed, publication-planning chat, questions asked "to establish authorship" and out of genuine interest.
- **Middling thesis:** "You (as examiner) are looking for 'excellent bits' to put in the report (to justify passing) and probably scrapping away at how to improve sections that are not quite as you want" (Z). Tone: longer, more persistent probing; the follow-up chains concentrate on weak chapters; corrections discussed in the room.
- **Weak thesis:** "so varied that I won't begin to generalize" (Z) — probing deepens and spreads; the viva can flip the result either way: "I have seen people salvage their PhD because they presented themselves and their work well under questioning" (interviewee V); conversely candidates expected to pass have failed (X).
- **Duration is a weak signal.** Short vivas can mean a clean thesis or a hurried panel; long ones can mean a long thesis or a difficult discussion — "both extremes are within normal range" (Thesisroom). Deliberation length is likewise uninformative: "The length of this discussion time is not necessarily related to the level of the outcome, so a candidate should not worry if this waiting seems a long time" (Cortazzi).
- **Pressure is delivered politely, not theatrically.** The norm is being "challenged in a pleasant but thorough way" (Poole interviewee T); examiners "should (and generally do) do their best to put the student at his/her ease" (respondent 14, Poole). Critique is framed as role performance, not hostility: "It is the role of examiners to evaluate: no thesis is perfect, and when they give critical comments, or express reservations, they are doing their job" (Cortazzi).

## 8. Question phrasing norms (politeness, hedging, directness)

- **Open-ended frames dominate.** Cortazzi's 60-question repertoire uses "What led you to...?", "How did you arrive at...?", "Can you explain...?", "Can you tell us more about...?", "How would you defend your methodology to a critic?", "What alternative interpretations... are possible?" Almost no yes/no questions; imperatives are softened ("Comment on the ethical issues involved...").
- **Warm-ups are disarming but loaded.** "Opening questions as warm-ups: they can be more probing than they may seem" (Cortazzi) — e.g., "What led you to this topic?" is really asking for motivation and judgment.
- **Criticism is wrapped in hypotheticals and conditionals.** "How would you defend X to a critic?", "But couldn't a critic say...?", "I wonder whether..." — the examiner voice is collegial, hedged, and indirect by design, consistent with "pleasant but thorough" (Poole) and with the aim of putting the candidate at ease.
- **One question at a time.** Multi-part compound questions do occur (Cortazzi warns candidates about "longer, more complex questions" where they hear only a couple of key words), but the default is a single question per turn, with the *follow-up* carrying the load.
- **Authority is signaled by content, not volume:** page references ("turn to page 45"), literature the examiner expects ("bring in wider issues... key work which they think is relevant and is not mentioned in the thesis"), and legitimate out-of-thesis probing ("examiners can legitimately ask about things which are not actually written about in the thesis but which the writer as a competent researcher would be expected to know") (Cortazzi).

## 9. How long phases run

| Format | Total | Breakdown |
| --- | --- | --- |
| UK PhD viva | 1-3 h, ~2 h typical | 0-10 min opening + "tell us about your research"; 10-60 min chapter/theme walkthrough (methodology and findings heaviest); 60-90 min contribution, limits, future work; final block wrap-up; 10-20 min deliberation (DiscoverPhDs; Thesisroom) |
| US defense | 90-120 min | 20-30 min public presentation; ~45 min closed committee questions; 15-45 min deliberation + outcome (Thesisroom) |
| Netherlands | exactly 60 min | 10 min lay presentation; 45 min questioning by opponents; 5 min to "Hora est" (Thesisroom; TU Delft regulations) |
| Germany (Disputation/Rigorosum) | 30 min - 2 h | institution-dependent (Thesisroom) |
| France | 2-4 h | 20-30 min presentation + extended jury discussion (Thesisroom) |
| Scandinavia | 2-5 h | public; opponents question in turn; stamina matters (Thesisroom) |
| Master's-level defenses | generally shorter | presentation + committee Q&A, commonly 30-60 min *(inference from the same sources' lower bounds)* |

A practice session realistically needs to compress: a 20-30 minute full run maps onto the Dutch hour or the arc of a US defense.

## 10. How the session opens and closes

**Opening (chair-run, ~2-5 min in reality).**
1. Examiners typically meet without the candidate first (~30 min beforehand) to align views and divide topics (Glasgow).
2. Chair convenes, introduces everyone by name and role ("the chair introduces you all" — Glasgow; internal examiner normally introduces the external — DiscoverPhDs).
3. Chair explains the procedure ("After the introductions, the appointed chair will explain the viva process... it will be repeated to ensure the viva remains on track" — DiscoverPhDs): format, rough timings, that the candidate may ask for clarification or repeats at any time *(the clarification permission is standard advice, Muller/Cortazzi)*.
4. Then either the candidate presents (US/continental) or the first warm-up question lands ("tell us about your research").

**Closing.**
1. The lead examiner signals saturation ("I think we've covered what we needed" *(inference; examiners "invite you to ask any questions you may have, and then bring the oral examination to a close" — DiscoverPhDs)*).
2. Chair asks whether the candidate has "anything to add" and any questions for the panel (Thesisroom; DiscoverPhDs).
3. Candidate is asked to leave; panel deliberates (10-20 min UK; 10 min-1 h possible — Glasgow; Thesisroom; DiscoverPhDs); in public formats the committee withdraws to a separate room and the decision is announced to everyone present (Cortazzi).
4. Candidate recalled; outcome delivered, feedback given: "the examiners give feedback and declare the outcome" (Cortazzi); if corrections are required, "you discuss a realistic timeframe to get the corrections done" (Glasgow); minor corrections typically 1-6 months, major 6-12 months (DiscoverPhDs).

**endOfSpeechPatienceMs = 3000.** Real examiners do not pounce on the first 300ms of quiet: wait-time research shows average questioners leave ~1s but that expert practice deliberately extends the post-answer pause to 3-5s (Rowe), and the viva literature treats unhurried thinking as the norm — "take a few moments to think. There's no rush!" (Muller), "please give me a moment to think about it... This is normal" (Cortazzi) — while examiners also use the gap to write notes. Three seconds sits at the patient end of Rowe's extended band while still feeling responsive in a voice UI, and it trains the exact skill the sources say candidates lack: tolerating silence instead of filling it.

**interruptionPolicy.** Grounded in the strong format rule that the talk is "a formal report" questioned only afterward (Thesisroom; DiscoverPhDs; Dutch regulation separates the 10-minute presentation from questioning entirely), and that hostile interjection is rare and officially disapproved (~4 in 60 vivas saw friction; chairs are mandated to keep questioning "fair and professional" — Poole). The Q&A escape hatch (soft bridge after ~60s) models the ordinary redirect of a drifting candidate without reproducing maverick behavior.

**responseStyleHint.** Mirrors the documented phrasing of real examiner speech: open-ended "How did you... / Can you explain..." frames dominate (Cortazzi's 60-question repertoire), warm-ups are "more probing than they may seem," critique is deflected onto hypothetical critics, and good examiners "contribute interesting points... rather than simply asking questions" (Cortazzi) — with Poole's evidence that tone should be "pleasant but thorough."

**pacingHint.** Encodes the two best-attested sequencing facts: panels pre-allocate topics and take turns (Glasgow; Cortazzi), and broad questions are "commonly followed up with more focussed questions on the same theme... sometimes in sustained exchanges of dialogue" (Cortazzi), over an arc that runs framing -> chapters/method -> contribution/limits (Thesisroom; DiscoverPhDs). It also respects the time constraint Cortazzi flags: "only one or two questions from each category are feasible."

**openingBehavior.** Reproduces the chair-run opening ritual: introductions of examiners by role, procedure explained "to ensure the viva remains on track" (DiscoverPhDs), and an explicit grant of thinking time and clarification rights — the two permissions the literature says candidates under-use (Muller; Cortazzi). Capped at 60s because in reality the chair "says very little except to give a brief introductory explanation" (Cortazzi).

**closingBehavior.** Reconstructs the real close: lead-examiner wind-down, the chair's "anything you'd like to add?" and invitation for candidate questions (Thesisroom; DiscoverPhDs), the out-of-room deliberation beat (10-20 min compressed; length deliberately uninformative, per Cortazzi), and outcome delivery in the genuine outcome ladder — minor corrections as the modal verdict, "almost all students who sit their viva pass" (DiscoverPhDs) — with strengths-first feedback, concrete revision items, and timeframes, mirroring "the examiners give feedback and declare the outcome" and the corrections-timeline discussion (Cortazzi; Glasgow).
